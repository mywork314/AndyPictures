No matter that the Rangers have
sometimes been accused of operat-
ing a baseball franchise like the Ital-
ians operated a war. Forgive them
their administrative trespasses,
their fielding errors. Forget the
price increases.

Nay, they deserve the Old-Timers
Humanitarian Award and the lasting
debt of the Old Box Score Readers’
Assn. Praise ‘em, they have resur-
rected The Giants, a lost civilization.

The Giants, or at least a shred of
them will parade through Arlington
Stadium Saturday evening and who
cares if Bobby Thomson’s short,
lethal swing cannot be restored?
Who cares if the field will be full of
bouncing bellys and sagging jowls
and creaking arms?

The Giants are back. Who knows
where they've gone since Willie
Mays’ fast legs and quick batting
stroke turned to stone? Since Sal Ma-
glie last knocked down a hitter?
Since Johnny Antonelli was the
Prince of Broadway?

Someone said the Giants are hang-
ing out on a spit of land near San
Francisco, but they hardly attract
any attention these days. Had a good
third sacker a number of years ago
named Jim Davenport who won a
string of Gold Gloves. Had a tricky
pitcher named Juan Marichal who
threw his spikes in a hitter’s face and
then came in low and hard. Had an
outfielder named Bobby Bonds who
was gonna be another Mel Ott. But he
moved on. They all moved on.

Funny about the Giants, if you're
up on your baseball lore, but they
always had a kid who was gonna be
another Mel Ott.

Return of the Giants

There was the Texas kid named
Clint Hartung who came after the
war. For a service team, he had hit
556 and threw no-hitters every day.
The Giants couldn’t wait to turn him
loose on the National League.

A year later in spring training he
was struggling to make the team. A
columnist for a New York paper
wrote of Hartung’s difficulties and
heartbreak.

The Giants broke camp and moved
through the midwest on a exhibition
tour. Everywhere they stopped, the
Hartung story would be reprinted in
the local journal.

Hartung had seen the piece maybe
20times and was sick of it. Finally, in
an Oklahoma City hotel lobby he
grabbed the New York writer by the
lapels and shouted, ‘‘When the hell
you gonna stop writing that column
about me?”

Andy burned out

There was another immortal of an
earlier time, an inspiration of the
team’s famous manager, John
McGraw. For years he dreamed of
having a Jewish star as a lure for
New York's growing Jewish popula-
tion.

Into his arms came Andy Cohen.
The Giants made room for this new
wonder by trading Rogers Hornsby
to Boston. His first game, Cohen won
the game with a double.

The next year, Cohen was gone.
The legend is that Jewish society dis-
tracted him with so many invitations
he could no longer concentrate on
baseball. As Cohen went to the
minors, Hornsby hit .387 for Boston.

The Giants always had a way of
disappearing.
