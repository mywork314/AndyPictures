Though he still does not mince words,
Froemming confesses that he’s gained
more finesse in handling angry players
and fans.

“In my younger days, it seemed as if
there was a hassle just about every
night. But you grow with the job. You
learn when to talk back and when not to.
You just develop a better feel for hand-
ling controversy. And you see that
anger fades rapidly, so you don’t get so
uptight about things. Let em have their
say and then tell ’em it’s time to play
ball.”

The press has always found him quo-
table. Once an Eau Claire manager
rushed Froemming on a disputed call
and roared, ‘‘You little. . . (expletive
deleted).””

In the blink of an eye, Froemming
ejected him. Afterward, the umpire
told reporters: ‘‘I wouldn’t have given
him the thumb if he’d called me a big so
and so, but little? I can’t tolerate that.”’

His long tour of the minors was inter-
rupted by a stint in the Army and a year
in which he balked at a contract offered
by the Northwest League. (Working the
league the previous year, he hadn’t
seen his family once from April to Octo-
ber). So Froemming wound up sitting
out the ’64 season, playing third base
for a Milwaukee softball team.

It was an uneasy time; a time of lying
awake nights wondering if he’d ever
again get an umpiring contract. But
keeping Froemming out of pro baseball
is as difficult as making water flow up-
stream. Froemming got a job in the
Texas League, where long distance
travel by car—600 to 900 miles between
parks—was common.

There was rejoicing in the Froem-
ming household when his contract was
sold to the Triple A Pacific Coast
League—the highest level before the
majors.

For an umpire, Froemming had said
repeatedly, there is only one place to
be—the big leagues. Everything else is
Poverty Row.

Can a short man make it as a major
league umpire? Not many have. Most
umpires are of average height or
better; some, in fact, stand very tall.

Tallness, society seems to be saying,
commands respect and lends itself to
authority.

Because of his size, Froemming hada
strike against him from the outset—
maybe two. And he’s no gazelle on the
field. Yet, as the super-ump of yester-
year, Jocko Conlan, said in his ’67 book,
“Jocko:’’‘I talked to some fellows
about Froemming. Andy Cohen, the
ballplayer, said: ‘He reminds me of
you, Jocko. He’s a nice boy, but he’s
tough!’””

How can the big leagues deny a fellow
like that? Not forever. In 1971 Froem-
ming’s dream came true. The news
came in a long awaited phone call from

Then 31, Froemming had served 13
long, lean years in the minors. He had
worked six leagues— Midwest, Nebras-
ka State, Northwest, Northern, Texas
and Pacific Coast—and had the lumps
to prove it.

His first regular season game in the
majors came in New York’s Shea Stadi-
um, where the Mets-Expos game was
halted in the sixth inning by a snows-
torm. That wasn’t so memorable,
Froemming says, but the rest of his
years in the big time have been chock
full of thrills.

He was working first*base, for in-
stance, that memorable day in Cincin-
nati in early ‘74 when Henry Aaron
stroked his historic 714th home run. He
won’t soon forget, either, umpiring in
the '76 World Series. And he was behind
the plate that September day in ’72
when Chicago’s Milt Pappas threw a no-
hitter at San Diego. But controversy
has a way of finding this gutsy
umpire—and that day was no excep-
tion.

Pappas was just one out away from a
perfect game—he’d retired 26 batters
in a row when, after working the count
to 1 and 2, he walked a pinch hitter. The
Cubs pitcher got the next batter out, so
the no-hitter was intact, but he had lost
the perfect game (27 straight outs).

Froemming, Pappas said, was the
culprit.
