Joe Soucheray

There are these old guys, old
sportsmen, who sit on the faded,
rock-back chairs in the sun at Hia-
watha Golf Course and they are
full of beautiful talk and memo-
ries. A lot of these guys have
cheated death three or four times,
so the sunshine, a chair on the
veranda of a clubhouse and some-
body to talk to is a big item.

Old ballplayers, some of them, like
Spencer Harris, the guy in the
checkered slacks and red golf
shirt. He played nine holes the
other morning and shot a 49,
which is good considering the
heart attack he suffered two years
ago that put him in the hospital for
the winter and reduced him to 107
pounds, When he played for the

Millers for 10 summers beginning
in 1928, he was called Slug or The
Duke because he performed at 145
pounds and hit home runs at the
rate of about 30 a year.

“Baseball was a vacation with
pay,”” Spencer Harris was saying
on the eve of the modern ballplay-
er's All-Star Game. “And if I had it
to do over again, I would. I gradu-
ated from Broadway High School
in Seattle on a Friday and reported
to Tacoma the next day. I got a
walk my first time up. My first hit
was a home run.”

Spencer Harris was born in Duluth
in 1900 to a railroad family. He
spent his first years in Minnesota,
his grade school days in Milwau-

kee and finished high school in
Seattle. By the time he arrived
back in Minneapolis to play for
Mike Kelly's Millers, he had been
up for two seasons with the White
Sox and would go up twice more,
in 1929 to Washington for a cup of
coffee and in 1930 to Philadelphia
for 22 games. He played most of
his career in the minor leagues, but
he would have been an all-star by
today’s standards.
