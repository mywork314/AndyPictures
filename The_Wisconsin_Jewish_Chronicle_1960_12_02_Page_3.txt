Wally Moses

When the New York Yankees
struck down the genial Casey
Stengel because he was “too old,”
they named Ralph Houk as the
new Yankee manager. Houk, an
aggressive and independent sort of
man, announced almost immedi-
ately that he was going to get some
of his own coaches for the club.
So he dropped Eddie Lopat as
pitching mentor and signed up
Johnny Sain. And then he hired
Wally Moses. Moses is not Jewish
and thereby hangs a tale.

Wally had a lifetime batting
average of .29] and was a veteran
by the time his career was over.
He had played in the outfield for
the Philadelphia Athletics and the
Chicago White Sox and the Boston
Red Sox. Not spectacular, he was
consistent and he was also good.

Moses had been good, too, in the
minor leagues. At that time, John
McGraw of the New York Giants,
wanted a sparkling Jewish player
to win over Jewish fans to the Gi-
ants. He remembered how Jews
came to watch Andy Cohen and
the feeling was that a Jewish star
would be a big box office attrac-
tion. Because of his name, Moses
was thought to be a Jew. But he
admitted that he wasn't. So Mc-
Graw didn’t want him. Asa result,
Wally hung around in the minors
long after he deserved to be in the
big leagues. Eventually, he came
to Philadelphia, and he was one of
the few players in the game of
whom it could be said that he was
kept in the minors because he was
not a Jew! Quite a twist. This
story has been told before in my
book, “The Jew in American
Sports.” But because Moses is in
the news again, I think it is worth
retelling.’
