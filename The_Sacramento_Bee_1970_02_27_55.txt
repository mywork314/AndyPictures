Giants’ Relief Specialist McMahon
Will Be Starting 20th Year In Game

By Tom Kane
Bee sports writer

CASA GRANDE, Ariz. — Relief
specialist Don McMahon, at 40 the
pitching patriarch of the Giants, will
begin his 20th season in baseball this
spring.

By this time relief pitching could
be called old hat as far as McMahon is
concerned. Almost all of his baseball
life has been spent in the bull pen.

McMahon, who resides in Garden
Grove, Calif., was assigned without
warning to a relief assignment in only
his second year in the sport by his
Denver manager Andy Cohen, vet-
eran and former major league coach.

“The Denver club knew I was going
to have to fulfill a military commit-
ment in the Army during the 1951
season,” Don stated, “and one day in
the spring Andy handed me a ball
and told me to join the relief crew.
And most of the time since that is
where I have been.”

McMahon was signed originally by
the old Boston Braves organization
and eventually helped the Milwaukee
Braves to win two pennants as a res-
cue artist. His travels within the
major leagues have taken him to
Houston, Cleveland, Boston, Chicago,
Detroit and finally to San Francisco
on Aug. 8, 1969.

The 6-2 right-handed hurler has a
lot of fond memories of the 1966 sea-

son when he was a member of one of
the all-time best relief crews.

“J joined the White Sox in June
and in relief the bull pen lost only
one game the remainder of the sea-
son,” Don recalled. “The bull pen in-
cluded Bob Larker, Hoyt Wilhelm and
Wilbur Wood. The one game we blew
was against Detroit and we all had a
hand in it. We had a three-run lead in
the ninth inning and could not hold
it.

“The White Sox were managed by
Eddie Stanky and he was one of the
best in determining when a pitcher
should come out or stay in.”

During the 1967 season Don won
five games without a loss and pitched
92 innings in 52 games.

While with the old Atlanta club in
1954 McMahon came under the wing
of veteran pitching coach Whit
Wyatt, who counselled Don to become
strictly a reliever.

“T could throw hard in those days
and I did not have what could be
called a good breaking pitch,” Don
stated. “I guess Wyatt figured I could
go along for an inning or two with my
fast ball. Later on I began using a
breaking pitch.

“T have learned through the years
that it takes just so long to get in con-
dition and it does not do any
good to rush things as I used
to do in the past. I always have been
