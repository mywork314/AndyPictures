SOME MONTHS ago, in this space, I commented
on the past career of Andy Cohen, the New York
Giant second-baseman in the 1920’5, the man who
succeeded, for a few years, the great immortal Rogers Hornsby, and who was one of the first Jewish players to hit the big time.
Andy is now the manager of the Indianapolis (American Association) club.
During spring training, some of the reporters talked with him about old times.
OF COURSE, they reviewed Andy’s career, but of some interest, apart from the statistics, is what
Andy said about the men who managed him. "I played under three wonderful managers during my active career,” he stated. “McGraw, Tris Speaker and Donie Bush. They were all great baseball men, and I profited tremendously by their teachings.
“As far as McGraw was concerned, you either liked him or hated him. There was no in-between. He was a hard loser, but certainly not a poor loser. I, personally, loved the guy.” Which is rather remark able, for Andy was let go by the Giants when he sported a batting average of .294—rather good at any time.

ANDY COHEN GETS HOUK POST AS DENVER PILOT
DENVER, Colo.—(—A familiar figure to Denver Baseball, Andy Cohen, will replace Ralph Houk next season as manager of the Denver Bears in the American Association.
Cohen's selection was announced last night by Bob Howsam, Denver, president and general manager, after Houk was _ named by the New York Yankees as a coach, to replace Bill Dickey, who is retiring.
Last season, Cohen managed Indianapolis in the triple A association and his contract was not renewed, .
The cigar-chomping Cohen led Denver’s Western league entry in that class A league to pennants in 1952 and 1954. The Bears wound up second in 1951 and 1953—the other years Cohen was here.
Houk managed the Bears three years. Last season Denver finished second to Wichita but won the Junior World Series playoffs.
Reports that Denver’s full working relationship with the Yankees may be altered by the choics. of Cohen, who _hasn’t been a member of the New York family, were scotched by Howsam.
He said Lee MacPhail, director of personnel of the Yankees, suggested Cohen for the job.

Kindness Means Information for Some Ball Fans
The old mental picture of a crooked gambler turned him into a beady-eyed individual with hat low over the forehead and cigar ashes dribbling down his vest. He was interested in the “fix,” the sure thing y #411 to bet on. And he was willing to pay.
Foamy “Baseball hasn’t that worry these “Anu days,” remarked Charlie Wagner, Boston farm system aid. “But there is another possible problem if not checked carefully.
It is the man after information. 
“A player up to the majors for the first time is bound to be impressionable.
And the kindness of 99 per cent of the fans is not misplaced. You know, invitation to dinner, etc. But the player has to be sharp. 
“A very, very few try to turn such moves into profit. They want to know clubhouse information about the players. Something not common knowledge which they could turn into the quick buck.”
That, according to Wagner, is the modern approach of the gambler. None of this “throw de game” stuff. Just a nice, pleasant, friendly approach of “What do you know that I don’t know.”
THE CONVERSATION also swung around to high standards of living for the modern baseball player. The meal allowance per day now is $10 which, said Andy Cohen, “is a far cry from one of my minor league teams. The players got a dollar-and-a-half and I got $3 because 1 was manager.”
Added Jack Onslow, the old Chicago White Sox, “yes, and today some of the players try to make too much money off that allowance and hurt their club by not eating properly .. . We used to notice that when the team
came off the road those who were getting “weak” by meal-pinching got some of that home cooking and started to play up to form.”
