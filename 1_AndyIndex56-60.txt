CLEARWATER — The Philadelphia Phillies work out at noon today in Jack Russell Stadium as pitchers and catchers continue to get ready for the rest 
the squad, due to report Tuesday.
There were more conditioning drills yesterday as the players moved back to the stadium after
a day at Athletic Field on Friday while the big diamond dried out, Yesterday they were able to
use the regular mound and infield for the first time since spring training opened.
The batterymen split up for a modified game that had them running to second, third and home for runs, Pitcher Henry Mason, who chalked up a 12-3 record working mostly in relief for International League champ Buffalo last season, had the distinction of belting the first spring “home run,” Mason cleared the left-center wall with a drive off Coach Andy Cohen.
Batting Coach Paul Waner, one of the best hitters in baseball history, worked with players in the
batting cage as an automatic pitcher fired balls plateward.
Monday the Phils go back to a 10 a.m, starting time for practice.

Tribe Brass to Confer With Cohen
Officials of the Indianapolis Indians will confer with Andy Cohen, new Tribe manager, at Jacksonville, Fla. where the annual winter minor league
meeting will open Monday.
Tribe President Ownie Bush and General Manager Ray Johnston left for Jacksonville last night and will huddle with
Cohen tomorrow. After his preconvention session, Cohen plans to return to the helm of the Chesterfield club of the
winter league in Panama. He will not come to Indianapolis until early in February.
One of the most important items at Jacksonville for the Indianapolis officials is to draft or purchase several players. While the Indians have a working agreement with the Chicago White Sox it is hoped to in-
crease the number of players owned locally. The Tribe owns six and have 21 others on the present roster assigned here by the White Sox.
Funds for purchase of players are being provided through the bargain ticket: campaign.
Fans are being offered a book of 30 admission tickets to Indians games for $25, a savings  of $8.
They may be bought over the counter at the Chamber of Commerce, and at Victory Field, or by mail.

Andy Cohen Due Here Tomorrow
Andy Cohen, the new manager of the Indianapolis Indians, is coming to town tomorrow for a noon press conference.
Cohen recently returned to the States from the Canal Zone where he managed the Chesterfield club in a winter league. 
It will be Cohen’s first visit to Indianapolis since his playing days in the 30s with Minneapolis.
Cohen, who managed New Orleans the last two years, was appointed to the Indianapolis job with consent of the Chicago White Sox after Kerby Farrell moved up to the Cleveland club. The community-owned Indians have a working agreement with the White Sox for the 1956 season.
The Indianapolis management also announced today that Joe Tipton, a catcher onthe club’s “restricted” list, has been sold to Birmingham. Tip-
ton quit the Memphis club in midseason last year after a dispute with Manager Jack Cassini and sat out the rest of the season at his home.

BASEBALL CHATTER
McGraw, Bush Inspired Cohen
By MAX GREENWALD, Star Sports Writer
Hollywood, Fla—Who had the greatest influence on Andy Cohen fm sgaping his career as a baseball manager?
The new pilot of the Indianapolis Indians, who officially takes the helm against Minneapolis at Indianapolis Tuesday night, instantly answered:
“John McGraw and Donie Bush.” 
Both, of course, are great names in the baseball world.
McGraw was the leader of the New York Giants during some of their greatest and Cohen joined that club as a youngster of 19 in 1926.
Bush has had a career stretching over almost a half century as a player, manager and front-office boss. At present, he is president of community-owned Indians Inc. Incidentally, Cohen refers to
Bush as “Donie,” the nickname by which he is known to all except residents of Indianapolis.
In his home town, it always has been “Ownie.”
“BECAUSE I was just a youngster with the Giants, McGraw made an indelible impression on my mind, I noted his disciplinary action, his handling of men and his way of playing percentage baseball,” Cohen said.
“But Donie made a profound impression because I was more mature when I was with him, I was better able to digest and absorb his methods of running club. 
Bush was manager at Indianapolis for seven seasons while Cohen was a second baseman. “I received my first experience as manager while with Donie,” Cohen continued, explaining that Bush several times placed him in charge of the club when the pilot would not be able to be present

“] learned manipulation of a pitching staff, from Donie,” Cohen said. Andy regards Bush as without a peer in handling of the mound staff. 
COHEN DECLARED that Donie 1s uncanny with pitchers, He was successful in the old Minneapolis field which was a hitters’ park.
“It is a matter of knowing pitchers thoroughly so that changes are not made one batter too soon or one batter too late.”
Handling of pitchers is one of two prime attributes a good manager should have, Cohen believes. The other is to maintain harmony among his players so “as to try to get the best from each man. You must determine the extent of a man’s ability, then get the utmost from him.”

The old mental picture of a crooked gambler turned him into a beady-eyed individual with hat low over the forehead and cigar ashes dribbling down his vest. He was interested in the “fix,” the sure thing y #411 to bet on. And he was willing to pay.
Foamy “Baseball hasn’t that worry these “Anu days,” remarked Charlie Wagner, Boston farm system aid. “But there is another possible problem if not checked carefully.

“A player up to the majors for the first time is bound to be impressionable.
And the kindness of 99 per cent of the fans is not misplaced. You know, invitation to dinner, etc. But the player has to be sharp. 
“A very, very few try to turn such moves into profit. They want to know clubhouse information about the players. Something not common knowledge which they could turn into the quick buck.”
That, according to Wagner, is the modern approach of the gambler. None of this “throw de game” stuff. Just a nice, pleasant, friendly approach of “What do you know that I don’t know.”
THE CONVERSATION also swung around to high standards of living for the modern baseball player. The meal allowance per day now is $10 which, said Andy Cohen, “is a far cry from one of my minor league teams. The players got a dollar-and-a-half and I got $3 because 1 was manager.”
