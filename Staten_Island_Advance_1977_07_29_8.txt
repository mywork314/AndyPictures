Tradition for the birds

By JACK REYCRAFT

One of the myths
which have been ac-
cepted as reality by
many of us is the
claim that the owl is a
wise old bird. They do
look rather intelli-
gent, which is proba-
bly why someone
wrote that poem
about the wise old owl
who sat in an oak and
listened far more than Jack
he spoke and asked Reycraft
why we all can’t do
likewise. Well frankly, I'd much rather
sit in an armchair and do at least half
the talking.

The fact is that owls aren't very intel-
ligent at all, As a night hunter, it all too
often lets out a cry of joy as it flies
toward its prey, and the prey hears the
warning and escapes.

In the Near East, many of the prov-
erbs label the owl a moron, and anyone
would be indignant if someone else com-
pared him to one. It isn’t clear who
started the opposite rumor in our civili-
zation — probably the same guy who
called mules stupid just because they're
stubborn.

Several years ago there was a screech
owl which nested in the eaves of the Sta-
ten Island Museum in St. George. It
stayed there for quite a while, but never
made friends with anyone. On the other
hand, pigeons are much friendlier, par-
ticularly when a group of children eats
lunch outside after a tour.

WEST BRIGHTON native George Gen-
ovese and his brother Frank (Chick)
Genovese are both scouts for the San
Francisco Giants baseball team after
careers as players and managers too.
Naturally they do a great deal of travel-
ing in their quest for talent. Chick now
lives in Orlando, Fla., and George and
his family reside in North Hollywood,
Calif. Just recently George was in El
Paso, Texas, and had a reunion with
Andy Cohen, who was a famed second
baseman with the New York Giants in

the late 1920s, with a batting average of
281. George was told that Andy Cohen's
wife is a descendant of the Van Duzer
family, which settled on Staten Island in
the 17th century and for whom one of
our prime streets is named.
