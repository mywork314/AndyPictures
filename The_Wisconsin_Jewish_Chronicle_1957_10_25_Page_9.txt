= By Harold U. Ribalow.
Bit of Jewish Sports History In N. Y. Giants Story

When Horace Stoneham, presi-
dent of the New York Giants,
showed himself to be a baseball
businessman rather than a senti-
mental fan by moving his team to
San Francisco, a bit of Jewish
ore history went along with

im.

Reams of paper have been spent
(or shall we say wasted) on the
transfer of the Giants to the West
Coast. Quite obviously, the Giant
management played a poor second
to the astute Yankee leaders who
have kept the American League
entry at the top of their circuit
while the Giants went from bad to
worse, Even with the brilliant
Willie Mays, the National League
team played to comparatively
empty seats. Stoneham said that
poor parking and indifferent pat-
ronage forced his move, He seem-
ed unable to comprehend that the
medicore Giant teams drove away
the old-time Giant fans and did
little to win new ones,

Things used to be very different
once upon a time. When John Mc-
Graw ran the giants, he had the
most spectacular wheel in town.
New York was a Giant town, And
McGraw tried hard to bring a
Jewish player to his club in order
to gain additional fans. In the
1920's, the National Leaguers
brought up Andy Cohen and for a
while the Jewish followers of the
sport made Andy one of the most
popular Giants around. But Andy
did not have the stuff to last. Be-
sides, he had replaced Roger
Hornsby, an authentic great, and
when Andy slowed down in the
field and hit less than .300 at the
plate, his Giant days were num-
bered. Of course, Andy's .280 av-
erage looks good today, but it
didn't please McGraw.

Danning and Arnovich

Later, there were other Jewish
diamond stars with the Giants.
atey Danning was a fine catcher
in Bill Terry’s regime, and Phil
Weintroub, a wonderful batter
but less-than-average fielder, did
lots of good work for the club.
Morrie Arnovich also worked well
for the team, as did Goody Rosen.
But by far the most solid Jewish
athlete to wear the Giant uniform

was Sid Gordon, who was both
left-fielder and third-baseman for
the Giants, Sid was a solid man,
a long-ball hitter, and a guy who
broke up many a game for his
team. When he was traded away
to Boston, the Giant management
issued a special statement ex-
pressing the club’s regret that it
had been forced to lose the serv-
ices of such an excellent athlete,
but that it couldn’t make an im-
portant trade unless Gordon were
included. Oddly, Sid’s days were
numbered after he shed the Giant
uniform,

But there is more to the Giants
leaving than remembering the
Jewish stars who cavorted on the
Polo Grounds baseball field. To
more than one Jewish youngster
who fell in love with baseball, the
Giants were important in another
sense as well. They represented
America, with the memories of
Matty and Mel Ott (who was
playing out the s in those
days) and Carl Hubbell and Jo-Jo
Moore and Fred Fitzimonns and
Hughie Critz. One took to these
players and one felt that one was
more of an American in following
their fortunes. Our parents accep-
ted that we were different and
that we were interested in some-
thing they could not fathom, To
this writer, it was the Polo
Grounds, not Yankee Stadium or
Ebbets Field.

They Were Good Days

The first game we saw at the
Polo Grounds was late in 1933,
after the Giants already had
clinched the pennant. They were
opposing the Braves, then of Bos-
ton and not Milwaukee. Ed Brandt
pitched for Boston and he was a
tough left-hander. The Giant
pitcher was Hubbell. Phil Wein-
troub was then a Giant outfielder
and we recall how he followed his
every gesture, Hubbell pitched a
four hitter and won 2-1. The
Giant team included, besides
Weintroub, Sam Leslie (who re-
placed after the flag was won, the
great Bill Terry), Hugh  Critz,
Blondie Ryan and Johnny Vergez.
Gus Mancuso caught and we re-
member wondering why Harry
Danning wasn't given a chance.
